# Where cost and quality diverge across New York hospitals

**To:** Health-plan network strategy
**From:** Meet Patel
**Date:** September 18, 2026
**Data:** CMS Hospital Compare + Medicare inpatient payment (Sept 2026 refresh)

---

**Bottom line:** When choosing or steering patients toward hospitals, **patient
experience is a usable signal of clinical quality — and price is not.** Across 90
New York hospitals, hospitals patients rate highly readmit measurably fewer
patients, while hospitals that cost more per stay are no better on outcomes.

## The question

Health plans and systems want to send patients to hospitals that deliver good
outcomes without overpaying. That requires knowing whether the things we can
easily see — what a hospital charges, how patients rate it — actually track the
thing we care about: clinical quality. This analysis tests which signal, if any,
predicts 30-day readmission performance across NY hospitals.

## What we did

We combined three federal datasets — hospital quality (readmissions,
patient-satisfaction surveys), Medicare inpatient payments, and hospital-published
prices — into one hospital-level view, then measured how cost and satisfaction each
relate to risk-adjusted 30-day readmissions. The analysis covers the **90 NY
hospitals** with enough Medicare volume (≥500 discharges) and complete core
readmission measures to compare reliably.

## The finding

- **Patient satisfaction predicts quality.** A hospital's patient-satisfaction
  star rating correlates with its readmission performance at **r = −0.52
  (p < 0.001)** — higher satisfaction, fewer readmissions. The relationship holds
  within NYC, the suburbs, and upstate separately, so it is not an artifact of
  geography.
- **Cost does not.** Medicare payment per discharge has **no meaningful relationship
  with readmissions (r = −0.06, not significant).** Paying more buys no better
  outcomes.

In short: a hospital's patient reviews carry real information about its clinical
quality; its price tag does not.

## Recommendation

Use **patient-experience scores as a quality screen** when building networks or
steering patients — they are public, cheap to obtain, and genuinely predictive.
Do **not** treat higher per-stay cost as a proxy for better care. The lowest-cost
quartile of NY hospitals includes many that outperform far more expensive peers on
readmissions (22 hospitals are simultaneously below-median cost and better than
expected on outcomes).

## Limitations (read before acting)

- **Price ≠ efficiency across regions.** Medicare pays NYC hospitals **~89% more
  per discharge than upstate** ($29,531 vs $15,600 median) largely because of the
  federal *geographic wage index*, not inefficiency. Cross-region cost comparisons
  reflect labor costs and patient complexity as much as care. The high-cost /
  worse-outcome cluster is concentrated in NYC safety-net hospitals serving sicker,
  more complex populations — this is **not** evidence those hospitals are wasteful.
- **Correlation, not causation.** Satisfaction predicting readmissions does not
  prove that improving satisfaction reduces them; both may reflect underlying care
  quality.
- **Scope.** 90 hospitals with sufficient Medicare volume; excludes low-volume
  facilities whose figures are statistically thin. Figures reflect the Sept 2026
  CMS refresh and update as CMS republishes.
